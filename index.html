Jar

A jar is the simplest answer to an old problem: how to hold something and keep it. These six were made between about 490 BCE and the 1700s, in Greece, southern Italy, Korea and China. They held oil, water, ashes and grain. Some were built for the kitchen, others for the grave or the palace. Read in order, they show the same shape carrying very different jobs.

  1. 1 · 1927.150c. 490 BCE

    Neck Amphora (storage jar): Nereus (or Triton) with Scepter and Dolphin; Woman Running

    Maker
    Attributed to the Berlin Painter
    Culture
    Greek (Attica), Archaic period
    Medium
    Terracotta, red-figure technique
    Size
    30.8 × 18 cm

    The amphora is the jar of the ancient Mediterranean. Thousands of plain ones moved wine and oil by sea, and this one carries a sea god holding a dolphin by the tail. The Berlin Painter left almost the whole surface black and set one figure on each side, so the curve of the jar becomes his stage. It opens the exhibition because it shows both things a jar can be at once: a container with a practical shape, and a surface for a picture.

    Collection record
  2. 2 · 1949.89c. 430–400 BCE

    Hydria (water jar) with Siren Attachment

    Culture
    Greek, Classical period
    Medium
    Bronze; body hammered from a single sheet, cast handles, foot and rim
    Size
    Rim diam. 15.7 cm

    A hydria has three handles: two at the sides for lifting it full, and one at the back for tipping it to pour. This one is bronze, not clay, and a winged siren sits where the back handle meets the body. Sirens were linked with mourning, and bronze hydriai like this were sometimes used to hold cremated remains. It is here to show the jar's quietest job. A shape made for carrying water could also carry a person to the grave.

    Catalogue entry
  3. 3 · 1960.367340–320 BCE

    Nestoris (two-handled jar) with Mythological Scenes

    Maker
    Attributed to the Choephoroi Painter
    Culture
    Greek, made in southern Italy; Late Classical to Early Hellenistic
    Medium
    Terracotta, red-figure technique
    Size
    65.7 × 48.3 cm

    The nestoris was not a Greek shape. It came from the local peoples of southern Italy, and Greek painters working there adopted it for their customers. At almost 66 cm tall it is the largest jar in the room and was made for show, not storage. Its body and neck carry four stories: Orestes hunted by a Fury at Delphi, Phrixos and the golden ram, a battle, and Aktaion torn apart by his own dogs. It is here as the jar as a meeting point between cultures.

  4. 4 · 1994.1641st–2nd century CE

    Round-Bottomed, Globular Jar with Flaring Lip and Two Pointed, Openwork Handles, and with Cord-Marked Decor

    Culture
    Korean, Proto-Three Kingdoms period
    Medium
    Proto-Kaya ware; grayish buff earthenware with appliqué handles and cord-marked surfaces
    Size
    28.8 × 23 cm

    This jar has no glaze, no painting and no flat base. The faint texture on its surface comes from the potter beating the wet clay with a paddle wrapped in cord, which pressed the walls thin and strong. The round bottom would have rested in sand, ash or a stand. It is here as the working jar, built for daily use, and as the earliest East Asian piece in the exhibition. Everything after it in this room adds glaze, color and display to a shape that already worked.

    Collection record
  5. 5 · 2006.170.264First half of the 8th century

    Jar with cobalt-blue splashes

    Culture
    Chinese, Tang dynasty (618–907), probably Henan province
    Medium
    White earthenware with lead-fluxed glaze splashed with cobalt blue
    Size
    18.1 × 21 cm

    Tang potters used cobalt blue sparingly, because the pigment was rare and costly. Here it was splashed onto a soft lead glaze, which melted in the kiln and let the blue run down the sides. The result is loose and unplanned. It is here as an early step toward blue-and-white, one of the most widely copied ceramic styles in the world. Compare it with the next jar, where the same blue is drawn with a brush and fully controlled.

    Collection record
  6. 6 · 2003.291Mid-18th century

    Large, Broad-Shouldered Jar with Decoration of Two Striding Dragons, Each Pursuing a Flaming Jewel

    Culture
    Korean, Chosŏn dynasty (1392–1910)
    Medium
    Blue-and-white porcelain, decorated in underglaze cobalt blue
    Size
    44.3 × 35 cm

    Broad shoulders and a narrow waist mark a jar type popular in Korea from the late 1600s to the 1800s. Two dragons circle the body, each chasing a flaming jewel. The motif came from Buddhist art and stands for the pursuit of wisdom. The exhibition ends here because two of its threads meet. This is Korean, like the cord-marked jar made some 1,600 years earlier, and its cobalt is painted with the control the Tang potter did not have. The jar is now a statement as well as a container.

    Collection record
